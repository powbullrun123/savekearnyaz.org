Kearny's Water Infrastructure

170 Years of Drilling for Water
in the Copper Corridor

From the first hand-dug holes along the Gila River to the deep granite boreholes that could save the town — this is the story of how Kearny's wells were built, why they went deeper than anyone else's, and what's been sitting dormant beneath the surface for decades.

Chapter I — Before the Town

Water Was Here First

Long before Kennecott Copper built the planned community of Kearny in 1958, the Gila River corridor was already known for its water. The Pima and Apache peoples had lived along these banks for centuries. When General Stephen Watts Kearny marched his Army of the West through this stretch on November 7, 1846, his lieutenant William Emory named the tributary they camped beside "Mineral Creek" — a name that foreshadowed everything that would follow.

By the 1880s, gold and silver prospectors were punching shallow holes into the desert floor along the creek beds. They weren't just looking for ore — they needed water to process it. The earliest wells in this basin were hand-dug, shallow, and temporary, built to serve the immediate needs of mining camps like Ray, Sonora, and Barcelona.

These first wells tapped the alluvial subflow — the saturated sand and gravel just beneath the riverbed. It was easy water. Shallow. Reliable in wet years. And it was this water that the Globe Equity Decree of 1935 would eventually place under strict legal allocation — the same decree that is strangling Kearny's water supply today.

Chapter II — The Copper Boom

When the Mines Went Deep,
So Did the Wells

The Ray Copper Company began large-scale operations in 1911, and by the 1940s Kennecott had converted the underground mine to an open pit. As the pit expanded, it consumed the towns of Ray, Sonora, and Barcelona — and all their water infrastructure with them.

Kennecott wasn't just building houses when they created Kearny in 1958. They were building a permanent industrial town — and a permanent town in the Arizona desert needs permanent water. The shallow alluvial wells that served the old mining camps weren't going to cut it. Droughts had already proven that surface water and shallow groundwater were unreliable.

So they did something bold: they drilled deep.

Kennecott's engineers understood that beneath the sandy alluvium near the river surface — the water governed by the Globe Decree — lay an entirely different geological formation. Below 400 to 600 feet, the drill bits hit fractured granite and volcanic rock. This wasn't river water filtering through sand. This was water trapped in ancient rock fractures — percolating groundwater — a separate hydrological system entirely.

They invested in 18-inch steel casing — massive bore infrastructure designed not for a temporary mining camp, but for decades of municipal-grade water production. At 1,150 and 1,250 feet deep, these weren't wells — they were engineered water vaults.

1880s – 1940s

The Shallow Era

Hand-dug and machine-drilled wells serving mining camps along Mineral Creek and the Gila River. Depths typically 50–200 feet. Tapping alluvial subflow — the same water the Globe Decree now controls.

1950s – 1960s

The Kennecott Investment

As Kearny was built, Kennecott drilled municipal supply wells to serve a permanent town. Multiple wells installed across the basin, some reaching 500+ feet. Water hit at ~100 feet in the 1960s — the alluvial layer was still generous.

Deep Bore Era

The Vault Wells — Going to Granite

Two wells drilled to extraordinary depth — 1,150 and 1,250 feet — punching through alluvium into fractured granite. 18-inch casing installed for high-volume production. These wells were built to be drought-proof. Today, they sit capped and dormant.

Chapter III — The Foresight

They Knew the Drought Would Come

Mining companies don't invest hundreds of thousands of dollars drilling 1,250 feet into granite on a whim. They knew. Arizona's cycle of drought and abundance was well documented even in the mid-twentieth century. The engineers who designed Kearny's water infrastructure understood three critical things:

Lesson 1

Surface Water Is Unreliable

Coolidge Dam, completed in 1928, was already showing the boom-and-bust cycle of the Gila River. Years of plenty followed by years of near-empty reservoir. The mining company needed water it could count on regardless of rainfall — water that didn't depend on the dam.

Lesson 2

Shallow Wells Dry Out in Drought

The alluvial wells that served Ray and the old camps dropped dramatically during dry spells. The water table in shallow alluvium tracks the river — when the river drops, the wells drop. A town of 2,000 people can't operate on a water source that disappears when you need it most.

Lesson 3

Granite Holds Water Differently

Fractured granite aquifers don't behave like alluvial sand. Water in rock fractures at 1,000+ feet is geologically isolated from surface conditions. It doesn't evaporate. It doesn't drain when the river drops. It's stored in a natural vault — pressurized, filtered, and persistent across decades of drought.

The Decision

Build It to Last — 18" Casing, 1,250 Feet

Kennecott invested in the largest casing diameter available — 18 inches — at depths exceeding 1,100 feet. This wasn't a drinking well for a ranch house. This was infrastructure rated for 2,000+ GPM production, built to supply a town through the worst drought Arizona could throw at it. That infrastructure is still in the ground today.
